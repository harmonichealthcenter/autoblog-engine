# Who Founded Qiari? Meet David Wong, the Biohacker Behind the App

David Wong founded Qiari.ai in 2024 as the device-free, app-based entry point to frequency wellness. Wong previously created the Qi Coil ecosystem and built Qiari to make remote frequency sessions accessible without hardware investment. The platform pairs structured session experiences with AI personalization that adapts to individual user intentions.

That is the short answer to "who founded Qiari," but the more interesting question is *why*. Wong already had a working frequency hardware business. Building a subscription app from scratch, in a category most people have never heard of, is not the obvious next move for someone selling premium devices. Understanding why he did it anyway tells you more about what Qiari actually is than any feature list will.

## David Wong: The Qi Coil Creator Behind Qiari

### From hardware obsession to app-first wellness

Wong's path into frequency wellness started the way a lot of biohacker stories start: as a personal problem. He was chasing better sleep, sharper focus, and more consistent energy, and he ended up deep in the world of PEMF devices, sound therapy, and frequency-based tools. That obsession eventually turned into the Qi Coil, a piece of hardware that lets users run specific frequency sessions at home.

The Qi Coil found its audience. Practitioners, biohackers, and people already fluent in the language of frequency wellness bought in. But Wong noticed something uncomfortable about who *wasn't* buying: the much larger group of curious people who wanted to explore frequency wellness but weren't going to spend hundreds or thousands of dollars on a device they'd never felt work.

### Why Wong built Qiari after Qi Coil succeeded

Most founders, once their hardware product is selling, double down on hardware. Wong went the other direction. He built [Qiari as the device-free version](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/qiari-hardware-required-device-free) of the frequency experience, running entirely inside a mobile and desktop app. The reasoning was straightforward: if the category itself is confusing to most people, asking them to buy a physical device before they've experienced anything is asking too much.

Qiari is not a downgrade of Qi Coil. It's a different entry point for a different type of user, built by the same person who already knows what the hardware ecosystem looks like from the inside.

## The Problem Wong Saw in Frequency Wellness

### Hardware creates a barrier to entry

The wellness market has a well-documented pattern: people are willing to try things that are cheap and low-friction, and increasingly cautious as price and commitment go up. Frequency wellness sat on the wrong side of that pattern for years. If you wanted in, you were looking at devices, coils, generators, and a learning curve steep enough to scare off anyone who was just curious.

Wong saw what a lot of hardware founders miss: the category was capped not by demand but by activation cost. There were plenty of people willing to spend fifteen dollars a month exploring something new. There were far fewer willing to spend fifteen hundred dollars up front.

### Most people want to try before they commit

This is where Wong's founder logic gets specific. He didn't build Qiari as a competing product to Qi Coil. He built it as the thing he wishes had existed before he bought his first frequency device. In his own framing, Qiari is the front door to the ecosystem, and the hardware sits deeper inside the house for people who already know they want it.

That reframing matters. It's the difference between "cheap version of the real thing" and "the way most people should probably start." Wong is explicit about the second reading. If you want the [full comparison between the app and the hardware line](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/qi-coil-alternative-qiari-comparison), that logic is spelled out in more detail.

## How Qiari Differs from Wong's Previous Work

### Qi Coil vs. Qiari: same founder, different strategy

Qi Coil is a hardware product with a defined price tag and a defined use case. You buy it once, you own it, you use it in your home. Qiari is a subscription. It runs on your phone. It updates. It personalizes. These are not small differences, and they reflect a real strategic shift in how Wong thinks about reaching people.

The [question of whether Qiari and Qi Coil are the same product](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/qiari-vs-qi-coil-same) comes up a lot, and the honest answer is no. They share a founder and a worldview. They don't share a delivery mechanism, a price model, or a target user at first contact.

### Why device-free matters to Wong's vision

Every hardware product has a shipping problem, a customs problem, an international pricing problem, and a "the user needs to be near the device" problem. An app has none of those. Someone in Berlin, Sydney, or São Paulo can [start a Qiari session from their phone](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/frequency-wellness-without-device) in the next five minutes.

For Wong, this isn't just distribution. It's philosophical. He's said in his own content that most people who are curious about frequency wellness will never touch it unless there's a low-friction way to feel something first. Device-free is how you make that possible.

### The role of AI personalization (Wong's newer insight)

The other thing Qiari does that Qi Coil doesn't is adapt. The app asks about your intentions (sleep, focus, calm, energy, recovery) and shapes the session experience around that. This is where [Qiari's AI personalization layer](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/how-qiari-personalizes-sessions-ai-layer) comes in, and it's a design choice that only really works in software.

Wong has been open that this direction, letting the platform learn from user patterns, is a newer part of his thinking. It's not a feature Qi Coil ever had, and it's one of the clearest signals that Qiari is its own thing, not a stripped-down version of anything.

## What Wong's Background Brings to Qiari

### Biohacking and frequency obsession as foundation

Wong is not a wellness marketer who discovered frequency as a business opportunity. He's a biohacker who got obsessed with frequency first and figured out the business later. That order matters, because the product decisions Qiari makes tend to reflect what an actual user of frequency tools would want, not what a marketer thinks would sell.

You can see it in small choices: session lengths that fit into a real day, intention categories that mirror how biohackers actually talk about their goals, [structured practice frameworks](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/qiari-daily-practice-15-minute-framework) rather than open-ended content libraries.

### Building for skeptics, not true believers

One of Wong's more useful instincts as a founder is that he doesn't try to convert skeptics through argument. He designs the product for them. The tone across Qiari's content, pricing, and positioning treats the user as a curious adult who will decide for themselves after trying it. There's no aggressive spiritual language, no medical overclaiming, no pressure to believe anything before you've experienced anything.

This shows up in how Qiari answers the [is-it-legit question](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/is-qiari-legit) directly, without defensiveness. Wong's position is that frequency wellness is an experience, not a belief system.

### The philosophy: "Feel it yourself, then decide"

If you had to compress Wong's founder philosophy into one line, it would be something like: try it, notice what you notice, and decide from there. That's why the [free trial and lower subscription tiers](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/qiari-free-trial-vs-paid-tiers) exist in the shape they do. The cost of finding out isn't the point. The finding out is.

## Qiari's Team and Wong's Role Today

Qiari is not a one-person operation. Wong is the founder and the public face, but the platform is built and maintained by a team that includes product, engineering, and content roles. Wong's active involvement tends to sit at the vision and product-direction level, particularly around how new session types, personalization logic, and the [practitioner tier for professionals](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/qiari-practitioner-tier-vs-subscriber-tier) evolve.

The broader ecosystem, spanning Qiari, Qi Coil, and adjacent education, is coordinated under Wong's overall direction, but each product line has its own operational rhythm.

## Why This Matters: Founder Philosophy in Product Design

### What Wong's values mean for Qiari users

Founder philosophy is usually invisible until you look for it. In Qiari's case, you can trace Wong's values through specific product decisions. The refusal to make medical claims reflects his position that this is a wellness practice, not a treatment. The subscription pricing at accessible tiers reflects his view that experience should come before commitment. The [step-by-step first-month guide](https://www.qiari.ai/blog/qiari-beginner-guide-first-month) reflects his instinct that new users need structure, not just a content library dumped on their lap.

### Transparency, iteration, and honest positioning

Wong tends to update his own thinking publicly, which is unusual in wellness. The product changes. The positioning gets sharper. Features that don't work get retired. If you compare how Qiari described itself a year ago to how it describes itself now, you can see the iteration happening in real time.
